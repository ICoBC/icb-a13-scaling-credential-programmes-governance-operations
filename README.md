# Scaling Credential Programmes: Governance and Operations

*From a successful pilot to a permanent service.*

## About this course

Your pilot worked. Learners enrolled, employers or departments noticed, and now leaders ask a new question: how do we run this for everyone, every year, without it falling apart? This course helps you answer that question with a governance charter and an operating model.

**Who it is for**

- L&D and corporate academy leads
- Executive sponsors (CHRO, CLO, CDO)
- Programme and micro-credential leads
- Professional bodies and sector associations

**Learning outcomes**

After this course you can:

1. Set up governance for credentials across an organisation.
2. Define service model, roles and funding for routine operation.
3. Manage portfolio growth and the retirement of credentials.
4. Report performance to leadership.

## Governance

### Why governance matters when you scale

A pilot runs on goodwill. One programme lead knows every credential, answers every question and fixes every problem by hand. When the programme grows to dozens of credentials and thousands of earners, this stops working. Decisions get made in corridors, two teams create overlapping badges, and nobody can say who approved what.

Governance is the set of decisions, roles and rules that keep a credential programme trustworthy as it grows. It answers four questions:

- **Who decides?** Who may approve a new credential, change one or withdraw one?
- **By what rules?** What standards must every credential meet?
- **Who is accountable?** Who answers to leadership and to earners when something goes wrong?
- **How do we know it works?** What evidence do decision makers see?

Good governance protects the value of credentials. If earners and employers cannot rely on a credential meaning what it says, the programme loses its purpose. This links directly to the VRITE pillar Impact: the programme needs to show that credentials are used and that they matter to the people who hold and read them.

Governance does not need to be heavy. A small organisation can run governance with one board that meets every quarter. A large organisation or a university may need layers. The aim is to match the structure to the size and risk of the programme.

### The building blocks of a governance structure

Most credential governance structures use a few standard parts.

**Executive sponsor.** A senior leader (for example the CHRO, CLO or CDO in a company, or a deputy principal in a college) who owns the programme's mandate and budget, and who speaks for it at executive level.

**Governance board.** A cross-functional group that approves policy and credentials. It usually includes HR or academic leadership, learning design, IT and data, legal or compliance, and a voice for earners and employers.

**Programme owner.** The person who runs the service day to day and prepares decisions for the board.

**Credential owners.** For each credential, a named person who is accountable for its content, assessment and evidence.

**Quality and assessment panel.** A smaller group that checks that credentials meet the standards, for example that learning outcomes and assessment methods fit the claim the credential makes.

**Data and privacy lead.** A role that watches how earner data is stored, shared and deleted. Check with your legal team which rules apply in your jurisdiction. <!-- VERIFY: add jurisdiction-specific data protection references only after checking with the course owner. -->

### Writing a governance charter

A governance charter is a short document that sets out the rules. It should be short enough that people read it. Two to four pages is usually enough. A charter normally contains:

1. **Purpose and scope.** What the programme is for and which credentials it covers.
2. **Roles and decision rights.** Who approves, who advises, who is informed. A simple table works well.
3. **Credential standards.** The minimum a credential must have: named owner, clear learning outcomes, assessment method, evidence, validity rules, review date.
4. **Approval process.** The steps from idea to launch, with expected timings.
5. **Change and withdrawal rules.** How credentials are updated or retired. You return to this in the module on growth and retirement.
6. **Escalation and appeals.** What happens when earners or employers raise a concern or dispute an outcome.
7. **Review.** When the charter itself is reviewed, usually yearly.

Use a decision-rights table such as this one. The letters stand for Decide, Advise and Inform.

| Decision | Executive sponsor | Governance board | Programme owner | Credential owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Programme budget | Decide | Advise | Inform | Inform |
| New credential approved | Inform | Decide | Advise | Advise |
| Credential content update | Inform | Inform | Advise | Decide |
| Credential retired | Inform | Decide | Advise | Advise |

Notice that every decision has exactly one "Decide". When two roles both think they decide, delays and disputes follow.

**Worked example: Charter for Northgate Group**

Northgate Group's pilot issued 12 badges for internal skills. Each team lead approved their own. After one year, two teams issued badges with nearly identical names but very different assessment standards.

The HR director (executive sponsor) asks the academy lead to write a charter. The academy lead proposes:

- A governance board of five people: the academy lead (chair), one HR business partner, one IT data representative, one legal adviser and one business unit manager, rotating each year.
- A rule that every badge must have a named credential owner, written outcomes, an assessment method and a review date before the board approves it.
- A quarterly meeting, with an online approval route for small changes between meetings.
- An appeals route: an employee who disagrees with an assessment outcome asks the credential owner first, then the programme owner.

The academy lead tests the draft with two business unit managers. They worry about delay, so the charter adds a target: the board decides on a complete proposal within 20 working days (illustrative).

**Worked example: Charter for Riverside College**

Riverside College has three faculties and a short-course unit. Each faculty already has an academic board for degrees, but micro-credentials from the short-course unit sit outside those boards.

The deputy principal sponsors a micro-credential governance group. It reports to the existing academic board, so it does not create a separate authority. The charter states that micro-credentials must meet the same academic standards for assessment as the college's other awards where they carry credit, and a lighter approval route where they do not carry credit. The quality panel is drawn from the existing quality office, so no new team is needed.

The lesson from both examples: link governance to structures that already exist, and keep the rules few and clear.

**Practice activity: Draft a decision-rights table**

Choose your own organisation or one of the two fictional ones (Northgate Group or Riverside College).

1. List six decisions your credential programme must make regularly (for example approve new credential, change assessment method, approve issuing partner, withdraw credential, set budget, respond to an appeal).
2. Create a table with roles in columns and these decisions in rows.
3. Mark exactly one Decide for each decision. Add Advise and Inform where needed.
4. Check each row. Could the Decide role act within the time your earners and managers expect? If not, move the decision.
5. Keep your table. You will use it in your governance charter.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Run the activity in groups of three, 20 minutes. Ask each group to swap tables with another group. The reviewing group looks for rows with two Decides or no Decide and for roles that have too many decisions. Debrief by asking which decision was hardest to assign and why. Typical answers: budget, appeals and approving external partners. Use the answers to link to the charter clinic later.
</details>

## Service model

### From project to service

A pilot is a project: it has a team, a start and an end. A permanent credential programme is a service: it must run every day, for people who expect it to work, whether or not the original team is still there.

A service model describes how that service is delivered. It covers what the service offers, who does what, how requests are handled, what it costs and how it is funded. Without a service model, scaling usually means the pilot team works longer hours until someone leaves.

Start by defining the **service catalogue**: what you will offer to whom. For a credential programme, a catalogue might include:

- Advice for teams who want to design a new credential.
- Review and approval of credentials against the charter.
- Issuing credentials and managing earner records.
- Support for earners and for people who verify credentials.
- Reporting to leadership.
- Retirement and archiving of credentials.

For each service, decide who the customers are, what they can expect and how quickly. These expectations are often written as **service levels**. Keep them realistic and few. For example: "Earner support queries are answered within two working days" (illustrative).

### Roles, capacity and funding

Once you know what the service offers, you need people to deliver it. Typical roles in a routine credential service are:

- **Service owner** (often the programme owner). Accountable for the whole service.
- **Credential design advisers.** Help credential owners write outcomes and design assessment.
- **Quality reviewers.** Check proposals against the charter.
- **Operations and support team.** Issue credentials, answer queries, correct errors.
- **Data and systems lead.** Looks after the platform, data flows and security.
- **Communications and employer or partner liaison.** Keeps stakeholders informed.

In a small organisation, one person may hold several roles. What matters is that every role is assigned to someone and that nobody holds a role for which they do not have time. Estimate capacity by volume: how many new credentials, how many issues and how many support queries per year. Use your pilot data as a starting point, and add a range, not a single number, because growth is uncertain.

### Funding the service

Pilots are often paid from project money that ends. A permanent service needs a funding model that continues. The common options are:

- **Central funding.** The organisation pays for the service as an overhead, for example from the HR or education budget. This is simple and encourages use, but depends on the sponsor's priorities.
- **Charge-back.** Units that use the service pay a fee, for example per credential designed or per credential issued. This links cost to use, but can discourage small teams and requires administration.
- **External income.** Fees from external earners, partners or sponsors. This suits providers who issue credentials to the public but brings its own obligations.
- **A mix.** Many organisations fund the core (governance, standards, platform) centrally and use charge-back or external income for variable costs.

Whatever the choice, work out the cost of the service in a simple way. List the staff time, platform and licence costs, assessment and quality costs, and support costs. Divide by expected volume to get an approximate unit cost. Present this to the sponsor as a range with the assumptions stated. Never present a single precise figure for a service that has not yet run at scale.

**Worked example: Northgate Group service model**

The academy lead at Northgate Group has pilot data: 12 badges and about 800 issues in the year (illustrative). Leadership wants 40 badges and 6,000 issues within two years (illustrative).

The lead builds a service catalogue with five services and assigns roles. The academy team of four cannot absorb the growth, so the plan adds one operations coordinator and part-time time from IT data and legal. The funding model has two parts: the HR budget pays for the core (governance, platform, quality review), and business units pay a small fee for each new badge design they request. The lead explains that this fee discourages badges created without a real need.

The lead also writes two service levels: new badge proposals receive a review decision within 20 working days, and earner queries are answered within two working days (both illustrative).

**Worked example: Riverside College service model**

Riverside College's short-course unit issued 30 micro-credentials in its pilot. The unit now serves all three faculties. Each faculty wants its own support, but the college has limited staff.

The service owner proposes a shared service: one central team handles design advice, quality review and the platform, while each faculty names a credential coordinator who spends a fixed number of hours per week on the work. Funding comes from a small percentage of income from each micro-credential, which flows into a service budget. For credentials with no fee, the faculty contributes staff time instead.

The service owner is open about the risk: if a faculty cannot release its coordinator, its credentials will wait in the queue. The service levels state this clearly.

**Practice activity: Sketch your service model**

Use your own organisation or one of the two fictional ones.

1. List the services you will offer in a table: service, customer, expected response time.
2. List the roles needed. Next to each, write the person or team who will hold it, and the hours per week they can give.
3. Choose a funding model and write two sentences explaining why it fits.
4. Estimate yearly volume for three things: new credentials, credentials issued, support queries. Give a low and a high figure and state your assumptions.
5. Find the biggest capacity gap. Write one recommendation to close it.

Keep your output. It forms the core of your operating model.

## Growth and retirement

### Managing a portfolio, not a collection

Success creates a new problem: too many credentials. A pilot with 12 credentials grows to 60. Some are used often, some never. Some overlap, and some describe skills that no longer matter. If you do not manage the whole set, quality drops and earners and employers struggle to know which credentials to trust.

A **credential portfolio** is the full set of credentials you offer, managed as one whole. Portfolio management means deciding which credentials to add, keep, change or remove, based on evidence and strategy.

Start with an **inventory**. For every credential, record at least:

- Name, owner and status (active, under review, retired).
- Date of launch and date of last review.
- The skills or outcomes it covers.
- Number of earners in the last year.
- Assessment method and evidence.
- Links to other credentials (pathways, stacks, overlaps).

Keep the inventory simple enough that it is actually kept up to date.

### Rules for adding credentials

Without rules, the portfolio grows by whoever shouts loudest. Agree a short set of **intake criteria**. A proposal for a new credential should show:

- A clear need: who wants it and why, with evidence such as employer demand, a skills gap or strategic priority.
- No unnecessary overlap with an existing credential.
- A named owner with time to maintain it.
- Assessment and evidence that fit the claim.
- A plan for review and, eventually, retirement.

Use a simple scoring or traffic-light approach, so that the governance board can compare proposals fairly. Consider also setting a **capacity rule**: the service can only add so many new credentials per year without extra funding. That connects growth directly to the service model.

### Retiring credentials responsibly

Retirement is a normal part of the lifecycle, not a failure. Skills change, tools change and demand falls. A credential that stays active long after it stopped being useful damages trust.

Triggers for a review include:

- Few or no earners over a set period.
- A scheduled review date reached.
- The content is out of date or the underlying skill has changed.
- The credential owner has left and nobody has taken over.
- A complaint, quality concern or change in requirements.
- Overlap with a newer credential.

At review, the board chooses to **keep**, **update** or **retire**. If it retires a credential, think about the people affected:

- **Earners.** They have earned something and should not lose it. Agree whether existing credentials remain valid, and for how long, and tell people what to expect.
- **Learners in progress.** Give a closing date and a way to finish.
- **Partners and employers.** Tell them early, and point to a replacement if one exists.
- **Verification.** Make sure that people can still check a retired credential that was validly issued. Decide how long you will keep records and how they will be stored securely. <!-- VERIFY: record retention periods depend on local law and organisational policy; confirm with legal before stating periods. -->

Write these rules into the charter before you need them. It is far easier to apply an agreed rule than to invent one while an unhappy earner waits.

**Worked example: Northgate Group portfolio review**

After two years, Northgate Group has 38 active badges. The academy lead runs the first annual portfolio review using the inventory.

Findings (illustrative): 9 badges had fewer than 10 earners in 12 months, 4 badges covered a software tool that the company is replacing, and 3 badges overlapped with each other.

The governance board decides: keep 6 of the low-use badges because they serve small but critical roles, merge the 3 overlapping badges into one, and retire the 4 tool-specific badges. For the retired badges, the academy sets a closing date three months ahead, tells all current earners that existing badges stay verifiable, and informs line managers. The inventory is updated and the decisions are recorded in the board minutes.

**Worked example: Riverside College portfolio review**

Riverside College's micro-credential portfolio grew to 75. A faculty proposes five new ones in one year, but the service can safely take on only three more with current staff (illustrative).

The governance group applies the intake criteria and scores the proposals. Three meet all criteria. Two overlap with existing credentials. The group asks the proposers to extend existing credentials instead, and gives them design support to do so. Separately, one credential taught by a lecturer who has left has had no owner for a year. The group gives the faculty 60 days to find a new owner. If none is found, it will retire the credential and tell the earners and the employer who sponsored it.

## Reporting

### Reporting that helps leaders decide

Leaders do not need every number. They need the few that help them decide: whether to keep funding the service, where to expand and what to fix. Good reporting turns data into decisions.

Start from the **decisions** your audience makes, and work backwards to the measures. A CHRO may decide on investment and strategy. A governance board may decide on approvals and retirement. A faculty dean may decide on staffing. Each needs a different view.

The VRITE pillar Impact asks whether credentials make a difference. Reporting should therefore go beyond activity (how many credentials were issued) to **use and outcomes** (who used them, for what, and with what result). Be careful with claims. Credentials can be linked to outcomes such as role changes, internal mobility or learner progression, but you must be honest about what the data can and cannot show. Correlation is not proof of cause.

### Choosing measures

A balanced set groups measures in four areas:

| Area | Example measures (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Reach | Number of earners, share of target groups reached, credentials issued |
| Quality | Share of credentials reviewed on time, assessment appeals upheld, earner satisfaction |
| Use and impact | Share of earners using credentials in applications or promotions, employer or manager feedback, progression to further study |
| Operations and cost | Time to approve a proposal, support response times, unit cost, budget against plan |

Choose a small set, around eight to twelve, and keep them stable from period to period so that trends show. For each measure, define it in one sentence, say where the data comes from and name the owner. Two people should be able to calculate it and get the same answer.

Think carefully about data protection. Report in aggregate. Do not show identifiable earner data to audiences who do not need it. <!-- VERIFY: confirm with the organisation's data protection lead which aggregation thresholds apply. -->

### Writing the leadership report

A leadership report should fit on one or two pages, with detail in an appendix. A useful structure is:

1. **Headline.** One or two sentences: how the programme is doing and what you need from the reader.
2. **Key measures.** A short table with this period, last period, target and a simple status. Use words as well as colours, so that the report works in print and for colour-blind readers.
3. **What the data tells us.** Three to five points, each with the evidence and an honest note on limits.
4. **Risks and issues.** What could go wrong, and what you are doing about it.
5. **Decisions and asks.** Specific requests, such as approve extra capacity, endorse retirement of four credentials, or agree a revised target.
6. **Next period.** What will happen, in brief.

Be honest about weaknesses. A report that only shows good news loses credibility. If a measure is poor, say so, and say what you plan to do. If you cannot measure something yet, say that too and describe how you will begin.

Match language to the audience. Executives want plain statements and clear asks. Governance boards want more detail on quality and risk. Avoid jargon, and explain any term that a non-specialist may not know.

**Worked example: Northgate Group quarterly report**

The academy lead prepares a one-page report for the HR director and the executive sponsor.

Headline: "Badge issuing is 15 per cent above target (illustrative), but review times have slipped. We ask for approval to add one part-time quality reviewer."

Key measures (illustrative):

| Measure | This quarter | Last quarter | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Badges issued | 1,610 | 1,380 | 1,400 | Above target |
| Proposals decided within 20 working days | 62 per cent | 80 per cent | 90 per cent | Below target |
| Earner support queries answered in two working days | 94 per cent | 92 per cent | 90 per cent | On target |
| Managers reporting badge use in development talks | 41 per cent | not measured | 50 per cent by year end | Baseline set |

The lead notes that the manager measure comes from a short survey with a 35 per cent response rate (illustrative), so the figure may not represent all managers. The ask is clear: approve a part-time quality reviewer to bring decision times back to target.

**Worked example: Riverside College annual report**

Riverside College's service owner prepares an annual report for the principal's team and the academic board.

The report leads with reach and operations: number of micro-credentials issued, share of faculties involved and cost per credential issued against budget (all illustrative). It then covers quality, such as the share of credentials reviewed on schedule and the number of assessment appeals. For use and impact, it reports progression: the share of micro-credential earners who went on to enrol in a longer course, and feedback from three partner employers.

The service owner adds a limits paragraph: progression data covers only earners who gave consent to be followed up, and employer feedback comes from a small group. The report ends with two asks: confirm funding for the next two years and endorse the new retirement rules for credentials with no owner.

**Practice activity: Draft a one-page leadership report**

Use your own organisation or a fictional one.

1. Name your audience and list the two or three decisions they make about the programme.
2. Choose six to eight measures from the four areas (reach, quality, use and impact, operations and cost). For each, write a one-sentence definition.
3. Create a table with illustrative numbers for this period, last period and target.
4. Write a headline of no more than two sentences and one specific ask.
5. Add a short "limits" note saying what the data cannot show.

[[?]] A pilot approach does not scale by effort alone.
****
Scaling needs a defined service, realistic capacity estimates and a plan to fill gaps. Working harder or waiting leaves the programme fragile, and outsourcing without defining services leaves nothing to manage. (Outcome 2)
****

**Question 5.** Which funding approach links the cost of credential design to real demand while keeping the platform and governance funded centrally?

[( )] Full central funding with no fees of any kind.
[( )] External income only.
[(X)] A mix: central funding for the core, with charge-back for variable costs such as new credential designs.
[( )] No funding model until the service is stable.
[[?]] The answer combines two approaches.
****
A mix funds the stable core centrally and links variable costs to use through charge-back. Full central funding does not link cost to demand, external income alone is not suitable for internal services, and having no model leaves the service exposed. (Outcome 2)
****

**Question 6.** Which proposal should the portfolio intake criteria most likely screen out?

[( )] A credential with a named owner and evidence of employer demand.
[( )] A credential that fills a gap no existing credential covers.
[(X)] A credential that overlaps heavily with an existing one and has no named owner.
[( )] A credential with a review date and clear assessment method.
[[?]] Look for missing owner and unnecessary overlap.
****
Intake criteria look for a clear need, no unnecessary overlap, a named owner, suitable assessment and a review plan. A heavy overlap with no owner fails several of them. (Outcome 3)
****

**Question 7.** A board retires four credentials about a software tool the organisation has replaced. Which plan best protects earners and partners?

[( )] Delete the credentials and records at once.
[( )] Stop issuing quietly and tell nobody.
[(X)] Announce a closing date, tell earners and partners, and keep earned credentials verifiable under agreed rules.
[( )] Reissue earners a different credential without telling them.
[[?]] Consider who is affected and what they need.
****
Responsible retirement gives notice, protects earners' validly earned credentials and keeps verification available. Deleting records, silence or unannounced swaps damage trust. (Outcome 3)
****

**Question 8.** A credential has had no earners for 30 months, its owner has left and its tool is no longer used. What decision is best supported by this evidence?

[( )] Keep it unchanged to avoid upsetting anyone.
[( )] Update it immediately with new marketing.
[(X)] Retire it, using the agreed retirement process.
[( )] Merge it with the most popular credential.
[[?]] Use the three pieces of evidence together.
****
No use, no owner and an obsolete tool together point to retirement through the agreed process. Keeping it unchanged harms trust, marketing does not solve the problem, and merging it with a popular credential adds irrelevant content. (Outcome 3)
****

**Question 9.** What is the best way to choose measures for a leadership report?

[( )] Include every measure the platform can produce.
[( )] Choose the measures that look most positive.
[(X)] Start from the decisions the audience makes and choose a small, stable set of defined measures.
[( )] Copy the measures from another organisation.
[[?]] Work backwards from the decision.
****
Starting from decisions keeps reports useful and short. A small stable set shows trends over time, and defined measures let different people calculate the same result. (Outcome 4)
****

**Question 10.** A report shows that 41 per cent of surveyed managers use badges in development talks, from a survey with a 35 per cent response rate (illustrative). What is the most honest way to present this?

[( )] State that 41 per cent of all managers use badges.
[( )] Leave the figure out because the response rate is low.
[(X)] Present the figure with the response rate and a note that it may not represent all managers.
[( )] Round it up to 50 per cent to meet the target.
[[?]] Report what the data shows and its limits.
****
Honest reporting shows the figure and states the limit. Presenting it as representative overstates the evidence, hiding it removes useful information and rounding up misleads. (Outcome 4)
